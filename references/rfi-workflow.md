# RFI-specific workflow

## The core question

An RFI asks for information. Before answering, establish which of four things it
actually is, because the response differs completely:

1. **The answer is in the contract documents.** Point to the sheet, detail, or
   specification section. Most common category, and answering it with a reference
   costs nothing and closes it.
2. **A genuine gap or conflict in our documents.** We owe a real answer, and likely a
   revision, sketch, or supplemental instruction. Own it plainly and quickly.
3. **A field condition or means-and-methods question.** The contractor is asking us to
   make a construction decision that belongs to them. Answer the design intent; decline
   the installation decision.
4. **A scope question in technical clothing.** The real subject is who pays. Handle it
   as a commercial matter, in coordination with the PM, not as a design answer.

Sorting into those four is the RFI equivalent of the triage tracks, and maps onto them
directly: (1) closed by pointer, (2) ours to fix, (3) cheap answer, (4) cost risk or stop.

## Spotting a scope question without becoming cynical

Most RFIs are legitimate. Contractors ask because drawings are imperfect and buildings
are complicated. Treating every RFI as an attack is both wrong and corrosive to the
working relationship.

That said, some RFIs exist to build a record for a change order. The tells:

- The question restates an obvious requirement and asks us to "confirm" it, which
  manufactures a document showing we directed the work.
- It proposes its own answer and asks us to approve the proposal, rather than asking
  what we intended.
- It asserts something is "not shown" when it is shown, just not where they looked.
- It asks who is responsible for something, rather than what the design requires.
- It arrives late relative to when the information was available, on an item now on the
  critical path.
- It bundles a real question with a scope assertion, so that answering the first reads
  as accepting the second.

The response to each of these is the same and it is not confrontational: **answer only
the design question, in writing, and decline the scope determination explicitly.**

> "The design intent is [X], as shown on [sheet/detail]. Determination of contractual
> scope and cost is outside the scope of this response and is referred to the
> Construction Manager."

That sentence protects the record without picking a fight. Never answer a scope
question by implication — silence on a bundled scope assertion reads as agreement.

## Answering well

- **Answer the question asked.** Adding unrequested direction creates scope we did not
  intend to create. If the question is narrow, the answer is narrow.
- **Point rather than restate.** A sheet and detail reference is stronger than a
  paraphrase, because the paraphrase becomes a new document that can conflict with the
  original.
- **Do not design in an RFI response.** If the answer requires new design, say so and
  issue it properly as a sketch, ASI, or bulletin. RFI responses are a poor instrument
  for design changes and they do not flow into the record set cleanly.
- **Watch for the cost-and-time trigger.** If our answer changes the work, say so in
  the response so the commercial process starts immediately rather than surfacing weeks
  later.
- **Note the response deadline.** RFIs carry ball-in-court dates and a late response is
  itself a delay claim. If we cannot answer by the date, say what we need and when we
  will answer, before the date passes.

## RFIs that reveal a pattern

When several RFIs cluster on the same sheet, detail, or system, that is information
about our documents, not just about the contractor. Note it. A cluster usually means
something is genuinely unclear and a single supplemental sketch will close five RFIs
at once, which is cheaper for everyone than answering them one at a time.

Flagging that pattern in the triage output is a high-value observation and one that
almost never comes from reviewing RFIs individually.
